    Rights on Paper, Shares in Practice: Women’s Inheritance from Colonial Custom to Contemporary Law in Lahore

    Abstract

    After colonial enforcement of agnatic custom, Pakistani inheritance law has developed through the Shariat Application Acts of 1937, 1948 and 1962. Furthermore, in 2011, deprivation was criminalized and a provincial Ombudsperson remedy was created in Punjab. Appellate judgments in 2025 and 2026 declared exclusionary customs void. This article then examines the extent to which this trajectory has enabled or constrained women in claiming their inheritance in Lahore. It combines a doctrinal history checked against statutory text and reported judgments with four semi-structured interviews with women aged approximately 30 to 60. When read against the law, the four cases were decided at four different points. Before succession opened, by a lifetime transfer to sons; inside the household after it opened, at the division of the estate; and nowhere because nobody contested. The article acknowledges that reforms are doctrinally strong but argues that they remain structurally reactive. They act on a woman who already holds a vested share and chooses to assert it while the decisive moments in these cases fell either before or after that point. The cases also suggest that a married woman’s claim is pursued from two households simultaneously. The findings are exploratory and are offered to generate hypotheses. They do not estimate prevalence.

    Keywords: inheritance; women’s property rights; Pakistan; Lahore; Muslim personal law; legal pluralism; hiba; Section 498-A

    1. Introduction

    In Pakistan, women usually do not receive their inheritance simply because they make a claim to it. Under Islamic law, and especially as Pakistani courts apply it, upon the owner’s death the estate devolves immediately to the heirs in fixed shares without any action by a court or a male relative. Despite this non-discriminatory fact, women rarely own property.1 In the 2017-18 Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey, 3 percent of ever-married women owned a house, either alone or jointly, compared with 72 percent of ever-married men; 2 percent of women and 27 percent of men owned land.2 A 2016 study by the Punjab Commission on the Status of Women found that women owned 10.6 percent of landed property only in Punjab.3 This distance demonstrated above that exists between a right that vests by operation of law and property that seldom arrives. That is the subject of this article.

    The key question, then, is: to what extent have historical inheritance practices and legal reforms from British India to contemporary Pakistan have enabled or constrained married women in Lahore in claiming their inheritance? The article is particularly invested in married women for two reasons. One, the same survey found out that only 19 percent of married women had been employed at any time in the preceding year, so most of them depended on their husband’s or family’s income.4 A married woman stands between her natal family and her husband’s household, with both parties having a stake in her inheritance. Nearly every protective instrument applies to a woman who already holds a vested share and elects to assert it showing how though reforms may appear to be doctrinally strong, structurally they remain reactive. In the few cases examined here, these turning moments became evident when, for instance, parents transferred their property to their sons either during their lifetimes or after their deaths. Or, in household negotiations in which dowry was either expected to be excused almost like an obligation or weighed against the goodwill of a brother’s or a groom’s expectations. This is significant because this history bears on the present through the surviving assumptions about inheritance. Considering the aforementioned, the article finally strives to make two contributions. First, it tries to consolidate the legal framework applicable in Lahore by using primary texts and corrects several commonly misstated points, including the 1937 Act’s exclusion of agricultural land the territorial limits of the federal 2020 property-rights statute and the framing of Section 498-A around the opening of succession. Second and lastly, it examines each case according to the stage of the inheritance process at which it was effectively decided. However, the identities of the interview participants have been kept anonymous at their own request for privacy.

    2. Legal and historical framework

    2.1 Shares and vesting

    According to the Quran, a son inherits the share of two daughters. A single daughter, then, inherits half the estate and two or more daughters inherit two-thirds; each parent of the deceased takes a sixth where he left children; and a widow takes an eighth where her husband left a child.5 According to Hanafi law, which is followed by most Sunni Muslims in Pakistan whatever remains after these fixed shares passes to the residuaries, namely the nearest male agnates. The Sindh Court applied this rule in 2025 to an estate that was left to a widow and a minor daughter. The residue passed along to his deceased brother’s sons, and his brother’s daughter, as distant kindred, took nothing from it.6 The Lahore High Court reasoned the same way in Kamal Khan v. Mst. Zainab, holding that a sole surviving child could take no more than half and that “the remaining half must revert to the collaterals”.7 Only when there is no residuary does the surplus return to the sharers and a spouse shares in that return only if no other heir exists.8 No such priority is given to make agnates in the Shia law and heirs of the same relationship to the deceased individual, male or female, agnatic or not, exclude more remote heirs alike.9

    The share vests at death. In Ghulam Ali v. Ghulam Sarwar Naqvi, the Supreme Court held that the estate devolves on the heirs “automatically, and immediately in definite shares,” so that “the brother, the father, husband or son cannot intervene as an intermediary.”10 It held that a woman’s so-called relinquishment of her share is opposed to public policy so that “the very act of agreement and contract constituting the relinquishment, was void.”11

    2.2 Lifetime transfers

    The only thing that is governed by inheritance law is what the owner holds at the time of death. An owner may give away his property by hiba during his life, and it requires a declaration by the donor himself, acceptance by the donee, and delivery of possession.12 Courts place the burden of proof on the ones benefiting from gifts that specifically exclude daughters. In Islam-ud-Din v. Noor Jehan, the Supreme Court held that a deed by which a father allegedly gifted properties to his sons was unproved because only one of its three attesting witnesses was produced and awarded the deceased sister’s share to her heirs.13 In Noor Muhammad v. Ghulam Haider, the Court held that the party claiming an oral gift that deprives a widow and daughters must prove every element of it by independent and reliable evidence and that courts must closely scrutinize attempts to deprive women through fabricated gifts, sham documents or manipulated revenue records.14 However, if a gift is proved and completed then it takes the property out of the estate altogether.

    There is one further rule that remains. A gift made during marz-ul-maut, a malady that induces an apprehension of death and in fact causes it, is restricted like a bequest.15 A bequest cannot exceed a third of the estate and a bequest to an heir is invalid unless the other heirs consent after the testator’s death.16 The doctrine is hard to invoke. Applying the Supreme Court’s four-part test from Chanan Bibi v. Muhammad Shafi, the Peshawar High Court in Bakht Zamin upheld a father’s gift to one son although the father was old, ill and died within weeks because no specific fatal disease was proved.

    2.3 Colonial custom and displacement

    Section 5 of the Punjab Laws Act 1872 provided that in questions of succession and related matters the rule of decision would be, first, “any custom applicable to the parties concerned” not contrary to justice, equity or good conscience and only then Muslim or Hindu law, as “modified by any such custom.”17 Colonial courts developed tests for proving custom and settlement officers recorded tribal customs in writing.18 Under custom, daughters could be excluded by male kin and a widow might hold only a limited estate that ended on her death or remarriage.19

    The Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1937 made Muslim personal law the rule of decision “notwithstanding any custom or usage to the contrary,” but section 2 expressly saved “questions relating to agricultural land.”20 As Bina Agarwal has observed of the Act as it still operates in India, that exception left the inheritance of agricultural land to custom and tenure law.21

    Promulgated on 15 March 1948, in Punjab this gap closed with the Punjab Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1948 whose section 2 applied Muslim personal law to succession “notwithstanding any rule of custom or usage,” without an agricultural-land exception. An estate that had opened under custom before that date could still exclude daughters.22 Section 3 of the West Pakistan Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act 1962 then terminated limited estates held by women under custom.23 In 1981 the Federal Shariat Court declared customary exclusion of female heirs repugnant to Islam; Ordinance XIII of 1983 inserted section 2-A into the 1962 Act; and in 2012 a Supreme Court decision that Batool and Zia-ul-Haq describe as “a dissenting decision” restricted that section’s operation before 15 March 1948.24

    In urban cities like Lahore, the houses and plots typical of city families made custom lose its legal force in 1937. Custom began to exist only as a practice from then onwards and Livia Holden and Azam Chaudhary show how social actors’ arguments about daughters’ inheritance travel into courtrooms, so that the everyday practice of law tends to perpetuate gender discrimination even where statute forbids it.25

    2.4 Statute and Constitution after 1947

    Section 4 of the Muslim Family Laws Ordinance 1961 gives the children of a predeceased son or daughter “a share equivalent to the share which such son or daughter … would have received if alive.” Its status remains contested: the Federal Shariat Court declared it repugnant to Islam in Allah Rakha v. Federation of Pakistan, but an appeal has been pending before the Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court ever since, so the declaration has not taken effect.26 The Constitution of 1973 protects the right to acquire, hold and dispose of property and guarantees equality before the law and the Supreme Court now reads inheritance disputes through Articles 23, 24 and 25 together with Article 2A.27 The Prevention of Anti-Women Practices (Criminal Law Amendment) Act 2011, passed by Parliament in December 2011, inserted a chapter of offences against women into the Penal Code.28 Section 498-A punishes whoever “by deceitful or illegal means deprives any woman from inheriting any movable or immovable property at the time of opening of succession” with five to ten years’ imprisonment, a fine of one million rupees, or both. Section 498-C prohibits compelling, arranging or facilitating a woman’s “marriage with the Holy Quran,” and its explanation deems an oath by a woman on the Qur’an “not to claim her share of inheritance” to be such a marriage.29

    2.5 Remedies available in Lahore

    The federal Enforcement of Women’s Property Rights Act 2020 extends only to Islamabad Capital Territory and the Federal Ombudsperson, and there it applies only to property situated there.30 In Lahore, the operative statute is the Punjab Enforcement of Women’s Property Rights Act 2021 and a woman who has been deprived of ownership or possession of her property may complain to the Ombudsperson Punjab, who may then refer the matter to the Deputy Commissioner for a report within 15 days and should preferably decide within 60 days.31 The Punjab Letters of Administration and Succession Certificates Act 2021 moved the issue of succession certificates from the civil courts to NADRA which processes applications against the deceased’s Family Registration Certificate.32

    2.6 Recent jurisprudence

    In recent jurisprudence the superior courts hold the most force and in March 2025, a full bench of the Federal Shariat Court declared customs such as chaddar and parchi un-Islamic and illegal, through which women were being deprived of their inheritance.33 In Abrar Hussain, the Supreme Court held that the state must ensure that every woman is informed of and enabled to claim her share and called for a proactive mechanism to identify and assist women heirs.34 In Noor Muhammad, it restored the shares of a widow and her daughters in an estate opened in 1955, holding that a mutation serves only fiscal purposes and neither creates nor extinguishes title and that while limitation may matter in some cases, the primary question is whether the alleged gift was proved.35 However, many issues still remain as apparent in September 2021, when a three-member Supreme Court bench led by Justice Umar Ata Bandial dismissed a claim by the grandchildren of a man who had, in 1935, transferred his property to his son and given his two daughters nothing; because the daughters had never challenged the transfer their children could not claim after their deaths.36 The ruling has been criticized for making a woman’s vested right lapse if she does not assert it in her lifetime.37 Table 1 summarises the trajectory.

The issue is most visible in the 2021 case because the estate had been emptied because of a father’s lifetime transfer without his daughters ever contesting it and so the right ended with them.

    3. Literature review

    Many scholars are in consensus that Pakistani women are entitled to inheritance but frequently they do not and many scholars differ on why this is the case. Historical work shows that colonial law fixed custom and made it concrete which is why it took Pakistani courts decades after 1948 to stop accepting a proven custom as a justification for the exclusion of daughters.38 The disinheritance of Muslim women in Punjab from 1526 to the present through court records and Urdu women’s magazines has been traced by Fiza Shahzad who also contrasted public discussion of inheritance with its practice. Holden and Chaudhary supply the most useful frame: custom survives its legal abolition by moving from the rule of decision into the reasoning of those who argue and apply rules.39 The empirical work in Punjab has shown that the dominant pattern is land surrender. Iram Rubab and Ahmed Usman interviewed 30 women in Mianwali, Rahim Yar Khan and Lahore and found that women commonly gave up their shares to male agnatic heirs and identified a patriarchal family structure, misinterpretation of religious commands, lack of awareness, stringent legal procedures and lower education as the major reasons for this.40 Javeria Khan, Asma Khalid and Adeela Rehman interviewed nine women and five lawyers and found patriarchal beliefs at work in both institutions and households.41 Amina Nisar and Rafidah Cusair interviewed nineteen women, six men and three legal experts and identified limited awareness, family pressure and norms that discourage women from claiming their right.42

    Relinquishment is treated as a third strand and where it is a constrained choice. Agarwal argues that without state social security, many women see their brothers as their main support after their parents’ death and because of that choose to forfeit their claims to preserve that relationship, while a sister who gives up her share is socially cast as a “good sister.”43 Deniz Kandiyoti’s concept of the patriarchal bargain names the general logic: women strategize within concrete constraints to maximize security.44 Momoe Makino adds dowry: Her survey in rural Punjab found that the value of a bride’s dowry is associated with her bargaining position in the marital household and her Indian evidence suggests that dowry may compensate for the absence of effective inheritance rights.45

    Newer works and studies ask whether or not reform has helped this prevailing situation to some extent if at all. Rubab, Beenish Malik, and Zujajah Bakht Aziz interviewed 12 legal, revenue, religious and women’s rights experts and concluded that little has changed in women’s land inheritance in Punjab since 2011, despite changes in digitized records and criminalization.46 Rubab, Rahat and Yunus interviewed forty women inheritors in Rawalpindi and Bahawalpur, and describe a convergence of private and public patriarchy. There was a mix of emotional blackmail and male relatives’ control over women’s identity documents which drove surrender at home and those who pressed on met courts and offices they experienced as masculine and unsafe. They also find that a claiming woman is often seen as acting for male kin such as her husband or in-laws.47

    This literature establishes the obstructions in women’s claims to inheritance. However, it does not clearly identify or state when a claim is rendered invalid or when it concerns parents’ lifetime transfers, as distinct from daughters’ relinquishment after death. The present article addresses these two gaps.

    4. Method

    In this study the doctrinal component traces the statutes and judgments in Section 2 using primary texts where they are publicly available. Where these are not available, reported summaries are used instead, which are identified as such in the notes. The empirical component of this article consists of four semi-structured interviews with women aged roughly 30 to 60 who had been through a parental inheritance. The study was designed specifically around married women. Of the four women, one runs her own business and serves as a contrast case and another described herself as lacking independent means.

    These participants were contacted via WhatsApp and interviewed by telephone. They were then asked open-ended questions about how they received or were denied their inheritance, what they knew of their rights, what family or social pressures they faced and what practical difficulties there were for them in this process. The participants were told the purpose of the research and that their participation was voluntary; consent was obtained and they were told they could decline any question if they did not want to answer. Identifying details have been removed and participants are referred to as Woman 1 to Woman 4. Telephone interviews gave participants a measure of privacy on a sensitive subject, at the cost of non-verbal cues. After this, each account was analyzed against the legal sequence set out in Section 2: transfers during the owner’s lifetime, vesting at death, division and registration of shares and enforcement. The aim is analytic because only four cases cannot show how common any pattern is; they can show how the law meets particular family situations and generate hypotheses for larger studies and so being statistical would make no sense. The accounts are retrospective and self-reported and were not checked against title or revenue records. They come only from women, so the reasoning of brothers and parents appears only as reported.

    5. Findings

    Woman 1 was her parents’ only child. After her father died she continued to live in the family home with her mother and other relatives but years later the property was sold and a new house built which is where she still lives. She described the property and her childhood home as never having belonged to anyone in particular. She never made a formal claim and did not know the legal process because she never needed it. She considered her experience unusual but still observed that Islam clearly grants women a share but that family and social pressure keeps many from ever getting it. Woman 2 received nothing because, before her parents died, the family property had been transferred into her brothers’ names so that, at their deaths, nothing remained in the parents’ names to divide and she was refused a share. She knew in general that women are entitled to inherit under Islamic law but not what her share would have been. She runs her own business and regarded the gifts and dowry her parents had given her as sufficient. Woman 3 also received no share but did not accept gifts or dowry as a substitute; she believed she was owed a portion of her parents’ property. She said that without independent means she lost her future security. The loss followed her into her marriage and her husband later asked why her family had given her nothing, adding to existing difficulties between them.

    Woman 4, however, knew she had a right to inherit and when the family property came to be divided the male relatives argued that they should take the larger portion and that the daughters should receive the share Islamic law provides. She took legal steps to protect her claim and also described a generational change. Women of her mother’s generation, she said, knew little of their protections and treated inheritance as an almost taboo subject for daughters. They accepted what their parents decided. She attributed her own readiness to act to greater awareness, even though she was worried that the old pressure still surrounded her claim.

    6. Discussion

    The most consequential case till now is that of Woman 2 and it shows the limits of reforms. Her share of the property never even came into existence because the property left her parents’ ownership while they were still alive. Section 498-A punishes deprivation “at the time of opening of succession.” The Punjab Ombudsperson protects a woman’s property and Ghulam Ali voids the relinquishment of a vested share. However, each one of these measures presupposes an estate containing the asset, and here there was no such thing. The only thing she could do was attack the transfer itself and argue that the gift was never completed by the delivery of possession, that it was made during a final illness that needed her consent and that a nominal sale was a sham. The courts have become receptive to such arguments and place the burden on the beneficiaries.48 But as Bakht Zamin shows, the deathbed route is pretty narrow and every route requires knowledge of the execution – when it happened and under what circumstances – and a willingness to allege that her parents’ last arrangements were truly wrongful and she had none. If it isn’t already difficult to sue brothers, impugning one’s dead parents is an even more difficult task.

    Time was also working against her: the 2021 ruling arose, as we can see, from a lifetime transfer of exactly this kind and barred the daughters’ children because the daughters had remained silent. Noor Muhammad treats proof of the gift as the threshold question and limitation as secondary. Although the law on delay remains unsettled, a woman in Woman 2’s position cannot assume that silence will preserve the claim for her heirs.49 Her case can be seen as suggesting a promising hypothesis for larger studies. As the moment of succession is better policed through NADRA records, criminal law, and the Ombudsperson, families intent on keeping property in the male line have reason to act earlier, while the owner is still alive. Reform focused on the point of death may partly shift deprivation into lifetime gifts but wouldn’t end it.

    Woman 1’s case can be easily misunderstood as the one where the law is working smoothly but it would be better to understand it as the absence of any and all contestation. A sole daughter takes half her father’s estate, his widow an eighth and each living parent a sixth; under Hanafi law the remainder passes to his nearest male agnates.50 Her account is consistent with a Shia family with no surviving paternal male relatives, with a lifetime gift to her (a practice noted among Sunni parents with only daughters),51 or with relatives who simply did not press their shares. In any of these cases, her mother held a share as a widow and it was necessary for there to be a later sale with the co-owner’s participation. The idea of undivided family property which other households use against daughters, protected her because no one was placed to turn it against her.

    It was Woman 4 in whose case the law worked as it was designed because she was aware of her rights and even acted on them. On her account, the male relatives conceded the daughters’ Islamic share and argued only over the larger portion. Any argument that could be made from custom was unavailable to them and so the actual dispute or issue moved to the terms of division where the law could act.

    The final question, whether women have been enabled or constrained still remains a difficult one. At best, it depends on the stage. At the stage of division and enforcement, the law has, in fact, enabled women and it has removed custom as a ground for voided relinquishment and criminalized deprivation at succession and has created a 60-day administrative remedy. A lifetime gift remains the principal lawful means of disinheriting daughters and every remedy after death waits for a woman to invoke it, at a cost that varies with her income and her place in her marriage. The Supreme Court’s 2025 call for a proactive mechanism acknowledges as much. The colonial past matters here mainly as a legacy because we know that custom enforced as law has not applied to urban property since 1937 but the assumption that property belongs to the male line survives visible in the preference for sons and the relatives’ claim to the larger portion. It constrains these women through expectation

    7. Conclusion

    The legal history of women’s inheritance in Punjab is one of steady doctrinal gain. In this history, custom has been displaced for urban property since 1937 and for agricultural land from 1948, relinquishment void since 1990, deprivation a crime since 2011 and exclusionary customs declared illegal in 2025 and 2026. In the four aforementioned cases examined here, these doctrines mattered only when a woman reached the division of an estate and chose to act on the rights she possessed. It did not matter much when the outcome was already fixed much earlier by measures such as lifetime transfers to sons, in household bargains over a woman’s dowry, goodwill or a husband’s expectations. Three directions follow from this. Research should now record when a claim is lost and distinguish lifetime transfers from relinquishment after death which would allow the displacement hypothesis to be tested at scale. Institutions could link death registration, heir verification and land records so that every female heir learns of her share and of the Ombudsperson when a death is registered.52 And law reform should scrutinize parental gifts that exclude daughters while respecting an owner’s right to dispose of property in life.

    A right that vests automatically must still be defended. Till now, Pakistan has largely settled what a woman is owed. What remains is how to deliver it and how that woman is meant to receive what she is owed. This is something that is yet to be settled.
